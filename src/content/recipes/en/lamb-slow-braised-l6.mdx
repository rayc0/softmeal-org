**IDDSI Level 6 (Soft & Bite-Sized) | 150 minutes | Medium**

Slow-braised lamb shank (慢燉羊腱) in the Mainland Chinese style draws on the rich braising traditions of Northern Chinese and Islamic-Chinese (清真) cuisine — lamb shank is slow-braised with cumin, broad bean paste (doubanjiang), dried chilli, Sichuan peppercorn, ginger and garlic in a deeply savoury, mildly spiced braising liquid for a full 2.5 hours. The lamb shank — a collagen-rich cut with connective tissue threaded throughout — transforms over this long cooking time into extraordinarily tender meat that falls from the bone and yields completely under fork pressure. Cut into 1.5cm pieces and served with its rich braising sauce, this dish meets IDDSI Level 6 (Soft & Bite-Sized) throughout. The warming spices and the lamb's distinctive flavour make this one of the most characterful soft-meal options for winter menus.

## Ingredients (3–4 servings)

**Main:**
- 2 lamb shanks (approximately 700–800g total)

**Braising sauce:**
- 2 tablespoons broad bean paste (doubanjiang, medium spice — or use mild version for sensitive palates)
- 2 tablespoons light soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon dark soy sauce
- 1 tablespoon Shaoxing rice wine
- 1 teaspoon ground cumin
- 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
- 1 small dried chilli (optional, omit for mild version)
- 1/2 teaspoon Sichuan peppercorn (optional)
- 4 slices ginger
- 4 cloves garlic, crushed
- 2 spring onions
- 400ml lamb or beef broth (or water)
- 1 tablespoon neutral oil
- 15g rock sugar

## Method

1. Pat lamb shanks dry. Heat oil in a heavy pot over high heat. Sear the lamb shanks on all sides until deeply browned, about 8–10 minutes total. Remove and set aside.
2. Reduce heat to medium. Add ginger and garlic to the pot, stir for 30 seconds. Add the doubanjiang and stir-fry for 1 minute until fragrant and the oil turns red.
3. Return the lamb shanks to the pot. Add Shaoxing wine, soy sauces, cumin, rock sugar, dried chilli (if using), Sichuan peppercorn (if using), spring onions and broth.
4. Bring to a boil, then reduce to the lowest possible simmer. Cover tightly and braise for 2–2.5 hours, turning the shanks every 45 minutes.
5. The lamb is done when the meat pulls easily from the bone with a fork and a chopstick slides through the thickest part with zero resistance.
6. Remove shanks. Strip meat from the bone, discarding bone, cartilage and excess sinew. Cut into approximately 1.5cm pieces.
7. Reduce the braising liquid over medium heat for 8–10 minutes to a glossy sauce. Taste and adjust seasoning.
8. Plate the lamb pieces and spoon sauce generously over each portion.

## Texture Test

**Fork pressure test:** Passes Level 6 — braised lamb shank meat at 1.5cm piece size yields under gentle fork pressure throughout; the connective tissue has fully gelatinised; no tough or chewy strands; the meat is moist and cohesive.

**Moisture check:** The reduced braising sauce provides excellent coating. Lamb shank is inherently moist from collagen content — ensure each serving piece has a generous spoonful of sauce.

## Safety Notes

⚠️ **Bone removal** — lamb shanks have a prominent central bone and small cartilage pieces around the joint. Remove all carefully before cutting into serving pieces.

⚠️ **Piece size** — cut to 1.5cm × 1.5cm. Even completely tender braised lamb can pose a choking risk in larger pieces.

⚠️ **Spice level** — doubanjiang can vary greatly in spice intensity. Use a mild (微辣) version or rinse off excess chilli oil before braising for residents with sensitive digestion or oral mucositis.

⚠️ **Lamb allergy** — while lamb allergies are uncommon, some residents may have cultural or religious dietary restrictions regarding lamb; always check.

## Nutrition

Approximately 310 kcal per serving (about 140g boneless cooked lamb), 32g protein, 16g fat. Lamb is rich in zinc, iron and B12 — nutrients commonly deficient in elderly residents. The collagen from the shank contributes gut-supportive gelatin. Moderately high fat provides energy density for residents with poor appetite.

## Cultural Note

Slow-braised lamb is central to Northern Chinese and Northwestern Chinese (新疆、蘭州) cuisine, reflecting the Islamic-Chinese food traditions that have enriched Chinese cooking for centuries. In Hong Kong's multicultural food landscape, lamb dishes have become widely popular, and many elderly residents — especially those who grew up in Shanghai or Northern China, or who worked in the Northern Chinese restaurant industry — associate braised lamb with hearty, nourishing winter meals. The warming cumin and spice profile is also considered beneficial in traditional Chinese medicine for "warming the kidneys" (補腎驅寒) in cold seasons.
