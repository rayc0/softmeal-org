# The Frazier Free Water Protocol

The **Frazier Free Water Protocol (FWP)** is a clinical approach that allows selected patients with dysphagia to drink plain thin water under specific controlled conditions, even when they require thickened fluids for other beverages. Originating from a rehabilitation centre in Louisville, Kentucky in the early 1990s, it has been adopted by speech-language therapists in numerous countries as an evidence-informed alternative to blanket water restriction for all thin-liquid dysphagia patients.

Understanding the protocol's mechanism, evidence base, patient eligibility criteria, and implementation conditions is essential for clinical teams managing dysphagia — particularly in Hong Kong's acute hospitals, rehabilitation wards, and residential care homes.

> **Clinical note:** This guide provides educational content on clinical protocols and does not constitute medical advice. The Frazier Free Water Protocol must be assessed and implemented by a qualified speech-language pathologist. Do not implement independently.

---

## The Problem It Solves: Thickened-Only Regimens and Their Costs

Conventional dysphagia management prescribes thickened fluids for patients who aspirate thin liquids. This approach is clinically appropriate in many cases: thickening reduces the rate of liquid flow, extends pharyngeal transit time, and provides more time for laryngeal closure — all of which reduce aspiration risk for thin liquids.

However, a strict thickened-only regimen carries real clinical costs that accumulate over time:

### Dehydration Risk

Patients prescribed thickened fluids frequently reduce their fluid intake, sometimes dramatically. The texture and taste of thickened beverages are rated negatively by most patients. Published evidence indicates that dysphagic patients on thickened fluids typically consume substantially less fluid per day than patients without texture restrictions — with some studies documenting fluid deficits equivalent to 500–800 mL/day below clinical targets.

Inadequate hydration in hospitalised and elderly patients is clinically serious. Published literature consistently associates hospital-acquired dehydration with increased incidence of urinary tract infections, delirium, constipation, pressure ulcer development, and prolonged length of stay. For frail elderly patients already at nutritional risk, this cumulative impact is far from trivial.

Studies published in *Dysphagia* and related journals have quantified hydration outcomes in dysphagic patients on thickened fluid regimens, consistently finding clinically significant fluid deficit risk compared to patients without restrictions. Systematic reviews and clinical trials examining thickener use have highlighted dehydration as a primary adverse outcome, prompting clinical bodies to seek alternative approaches for appropriate patients.

### Quality of Life

The capacity to drink plain water — to quench thirst as it arises, to take medication with a natural swallow, to participate in social settings without conspicuous dietary restriction — has significant quality-of-life implications for patients. Qualitative research exploring patient experience of dysphagia management identifies fluid restriction as among the most distressing aspects of a dysphagia diagnosis.

For rehabilitation patients working toward functional recovery, the psychological and motivational impact of a thickened-only regimen should not be underestimated. Perceived lack of progress, social isolation during mealtimes, and reduced enjoyment of fluid intake can affect engagement with rehabilitation and overall wellbeing.

### The Core Mechanism Argument

The central clinical argument underlying the Frazier Protocol is that **the pathogenic risk of aspirating small amounts of clean water is substantially lower than the risk of aspirating thickened fluids or oral secretions laden with oral bacteria**.

Plain water is sterile (or near-sterile). When aspirated in small volumes in a patient with intact pulmonary reserve and good immune function, water is readily absorbed through the alveolar membrane or cleared by pulmonary macrophages without causing significant infection. The real driver of aspiration pneumonia is not the water itself, but the bacterial load carried with it.

By contrast, oral secretions containing colonised bacteria — particularly in patients with poor oral hygiene — are the predominant substrate of aspiration pneumonia pathogenesis. This mechanism is well-supported by the oral hygiene intervention literature, including clinical trials demonstrating that oral bacteria density reduction corresponds to significant aspiration pneumonia incidence reduction.

This mechanism is what makes oral hygiene the non-negotiable prerequisite of the Frazier Protocol: without rigorous oral hygiene before every episode of thin water intake, the safety rationale collapses.

## Evidence Base

Multiple published studies and systematic reviews have evaluated outcomes in patients managed under the Frazier Free Water Protocol:

### Retrospective Cohort Evidence

Retrospective studies of hospital and rehabilitation patients managed with the FWP — published in peer-reviewed dysphagia journals — have generally found no significant increase in aspiration pneumonia incidence compared to matched controls on thickened-only regimens, when the protocol is implemented with appropriate patient selection and strict oral hygiene adherence.

One frequently cited retrospective series from a rehabilitation setting found no pulmonary complications attributable to free water access over a multi-year implementation period, with the caveat that the study was conducted in an inpatient setting with high oral care compliance and close monitoring.

### Systematic Review Evidence

A systematic review published in *Dysphagia* in 2017 synthesised available evidence on free water protocols, concluding that aspiration pneumonia rate was not significantly elevated in carefully selected patients managed with the FWP compared to those on thickened-only regimens. The review identified consistent positive effects on quality of life and hydration status.

The review also highlighted important limitations: most studies were conducted in rehabilitation settings with strong oral hygiene compliance, limiting direct extrapolation to community or residential care settings where oral hygiene standards vary.

### Randomised Controlled Trial Evidence

Evidence from randomised controlled trials specifically testing the FWP against thickened-only regimens remains limited. Clinical advisory notes that conducting high-quality RCTs in this population is logistically challenging, and current evidence is weighted toward observational designs. Future research may refine patient selection criteria and minimum oral hygiene standards as the evidence base matures.

### What the Evidence Supports

Current evidence supports the following conclusions:
1. In **appropriately selected patients** (see below), thin water access under protocol conditions does not significantly increase aspiration pneumonia risk
2. The FWP **improves hydration outcomes and quality of life** in eligible patients
3. The safety of the protocol is **critically dependent on oral hygiene adherence** — studies with poor compliance data are not reliably generalisable
4. The protocol is **not appropriate** for patients with compromised immune function, active respiratory infection, or inability to maintain required oral hygiene

## Eligibility Criteria: Who Can Use the Protocol

Patient selection is the most clinically critical component of FWP implementation. The protocol is not appropriate for all patients who aspirate thin liquids. Standard eligibility criteria (confirmed by the treating SLP) include:

### Inclusion Criteria

- **Aspirates thin liquids but not thickened fluids:** The core indication is thin liquid aspiration in a patient who handles thicker textures safely. If the patient also significantly aspirates thickened fluids, the protocol does not apply.
- **Medically stable:** No acute respiratory infection, no active aspiration pneumonia episode, no acute decompensated illness
- **Intact or manageable cough and immune function:** Patient can clear small aspirated volumes without developing infection. This requires clinical judgement about pulmonary reserve and immune status.
- **Oral hygiene is achievable and verifiable:** Either the patient maintains good oral hygiene independently, or a carer can reliably perform the required oral care before each water intake episode
- **Cognitive and physical cooperation:** Patient is cognitively able to follow protocol conditions and understand the restriction (water only under these conditions, not other thin liquids), or a trained carer can enforce the conditions

### Exclusion Criteria

- Active chest infection or pneumonitis
- Severely immunocompromised state (e.g., active chemotherapy, haematological malignancy, high-dose corticosteroids)
- Poor oral hygiene that cannot be reliably corrected
- Significant aspiration of thickened fluids on instrumental assessment (VFSS/FEES)
- Dementia with insufficient understanding to cooperate with conditions and without a carer who can ensure protocol adherence
- Patients who cannot maintain upright seated position
- Structural head and neck abnormalities that increase aspiration risk beyond what standard oral hygiene can mitigate

## The Five Protocol Conditions

The safety of the Frazier Protocol depends on strict adherence to five conditions at every episode of thin water intake. All five must be met; partial adherence is not acceptable:

### Condition 1: Fully Upright Position

The patient must be seated at 90° or higher when drinking water. This is not negotiable:
- Swallowing mechanics are most protective in the fully upright position
- Aspiration risk increases significantly with posterior tilt
- Water access in reclined chairs, wheelchairs at partial recline, or in bed is not permitted under the protocol

### Condition 2: Oral Hygiene Before Every Drinking Episode

Immediately before drinking thin water, the patient must have:
- Teeth brushed (or dentures cleaned and rinsed if relevant)
- Tongue cleaned with a toothbrush or tongue scraper
- Any oral secretions or food residue removed with a swab or rinse

This step must be completed before each episode of water intake, not just once at the start of the day. The mechanism of the protocol's safety is entirely dependent on the bacterial load in the oral cavity being minimised at the point of water consumption. For detailed oral hygiene technique, see [oral hygiene for carers: dysphagia guide](/en/caregiver-oral-hygiene-dysphagia/).

### Condition 3: Small Sips Only

The patient drinks in controlled small sips — no gulping, no cup tilted to pour a large volume. A small-volume cup or a spoon may be used to help with pacing if needed. The protocol is for controlled, supervised sipping, not unrestricted free drinking.

### Condition 4: No Thin Water Within 30 Minutes of Lying Down

After any water intake under the protocol, the patient must remain upright for at least 30 minutes. Aspiration risk increases substantially in the supine position, where aspirated material can access lower airways more readily. Staff must ensure scheduled rest periods and sleep times do not follow immediately after protocol water access.

### Condition 5: 30-Minute Post-Meal Wait Before Resuming Water Protocol Access

After any meal or food intake, a 30-minute interval should elapse before the patient may access thin water under the protocol. This reduces the risk of food residue and water combining to form a mixed-consistency bolus, which is more difficult to manage and more pathogenic if aspirated.

## Application in Hong Kong

### Acute Hospital Settings

In Hong Kong public hospitals, the FWP is applied selectively by SLT teams in rehabilitation wards, particularly for stroke patients who are medically stable and motivated to maintain quality of life during recovery. It is not a universal discharge policy; each patient requires individual SLT assessment and written protocol documentation.

The Hospital Authority's Geriatric Day Hospitals and Community Geriatric Assessment Teams (CGAT) may continue FWP management after discharge, coordinating with community nursing or family carers to ensure oral hygiene compliance. However, community-based implementation is only appropriate when a reliable carer is identified, trained, and committed to the oral care component.

### Residential Care Homes (RCHEs)

Care home implementation is more challenging than inpatient rehabilitation settings due to:

- **Variable oral care capacity:** Oral care quality varies widely across care workers, shifts, and facilities. Without a reliable standard of pre-drinking oral hygiene, the protocol's safety rationale is undermined.
- **Higher dementia prevalence:** Many RCHE residents have significant cognitive impairment. Residents who cannot cooperate with protocol conditions or communicate distress are at higher risk, and the cognitive-cooperation criterion for eligibility may not be met.
- **Staffing ratios:** The 30-minute wait conditions and oral care requirements create a monitoring and staffing demand that not all facilities can consistently meet.

The FWP in RCHE settings is most appropriate for **ambulant, cognitively intact residents** who maintain reasonable independent oral hygiene, who aspirate thin liquids but not thickened fluids on assessment, and who are medically stable. Written protocols, staff training, and incident monitoring are all required components of any RCHE implementation.

### Cultural Context

In Hong Kong's Cantonese culture, drinking tea — including Chinese teas such as chrysanthemum (菊花茶) and pu-erh — is deeply embedded in daily life and social interaction. For many elderly patients, the prospect of never drinking a cup of tea again carries considerable emotional weight beyond simple fluid preference.

For selected eligible patients, the Frazier Protocol may allow supervised thin-consistency tea access as part of a quality-of-life-informed care plan, provided all protocol conditions are met. This requires explicit SLT evaluation and written documentation; caregivers must understand that this applies to tea specifically within the protocol conditions, not to all thin liquids at all times.

## Implementation Framework for Institutions

For hospitals or care homes wishing to implement the FWP systematically:

### 1. SLT Assessment and Eligibility Confirmation

All protocol candidates require formal SLP evaluation, including:
- Review of medical history, current medications, and pulmonary status
- Assessment of oral hygiene status and achievability
- Ideally, instrumental assessment (FEES or VFSS) to confirm aspiration of thin liquids but not thickened fluids
- Documentation of eligibility and specific conditions applicable to the patient

### 2. Written Protocol Documentation

A written protocol must be placed in the patient's care record specifying:
- Which patient, under which conditions, may access thin water
- The five protocol conditions in plain language
- The name and contact of the responsible SLP
- The monitoring and incident reporting requirements

### 3. Staff Training

All clinical, nursing, and care staff involved in the patient's daily care must understand:
- That the patient has specific permission for thin water under conditions, but NOT for other thin liquids
- The five conditions and the requirement for oral hygiene before every water access
- How to respond to signs of aspiration (coughing, voice change, respiratory symptoms)
- When and how to activate the incident reporting pathway

### 4. Oral Hygiene Protocol Establishment

The protocol should specify the pre-water oral care routine, who is responsible for performing it on each shift, and how it is documented. Without systematic monitoring of oral care delivery, the protocol's safety cannot be assured.

### 5. Ongoing Monitoring and Review

- Any episode of coughing, voice change, or respiratory symptoms associated with water intake triggers SLT review
- Routine SLT reassessment at discharge planning and at significant clinical change
- Any acute respiratory illness or suspected aspiration pneumonia requires protocol suspension pending SLT review

## Common Misconceptions

**"If the FWP is in place, we don't need to thicken anything."**
Incorrect. The protocol applies only to plain thin water under the five conditions. All other beverages — juices, soups, nutritional supplements, tea, coffee — continue to be thickened to the prescribed IDDSI level.

**"Any patient who aspirates thin liquids can use the protocol."**
Incorrect. Patient selection is critical. Patients with active infection, immunocompromise, inability to cooperate, poor oral hygiene, or aspiration of thickened fluids are not candidates. For a broader framework of thin fluid management, see [hydration management in thickened fluid regimens](/en/hydration-thickened-fluids/).

**"Oral hygiene just before the first drink of the day is enough."**
Incorrect. Oral hygiene must precede every episode of thin water intake throughout the day. Oral bacterial colonisation recovers within hours; a morning brush does not protect an afternoon water intake.

**"The 3-ounce Yale screen passing means the patient automatically qualifies for the FWP."**
Incorrect. The Yale Swallow Protocol is a bedside screening instrument for initial aspiration risk detection. FWP eligibility requires a full SLT assessment, confirmation that the five conditions can be met, and ideally instrumental assessment.

## Relationship to IDDSI

The International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI) classifies thin water as **IDDSI Level 0 (Thin)**. Under standard IDDSI-based dysphagia management, a patient prescribed IDDSI Level 2 or above for fluids would not be given Level 0 thin water.

The Frazier Free Water Protocol operates as a **defined exception** to the standard IDDSI prescription — explicitly allowing Level 0 water access under specified conditions for eligible patients, while all other fluids remain at the prescribed IDDSI level. This exception must be clearly documented to prevent confusion among care staff who may be familiar with IDDSI but not the specific protocol application. See [IDDSI Level 0 — thin liquids: clinical overview](/en/iddsi-level-0-thin-liquids/) for context.

## Clinical FAQ

**Q: Does the FWP eliminate aspiration risk entirely?**

No. Patients on the FWP continue to aspirate thin water to some extent. The protocol manages risk to an acceptable clinical level by ensuring the aspirated material (clean water) is of minimal pathogenic consequence. Complete elimination of aspiration risk is not the goal; quality-of-life-informed risk management is.

**Q: What should we do if a patient on the FWP develops a respiratory infection?**

Suspend the protocol immediately. NPO for thin water until the acute illness resolves and SLT review is completed. A chest infection in a patient on the FWP should trigger reassessment of eligibility, not continuation of the protocol during illness.

**Q: Can the FWP be used with patients who have a PEG or NGT?**

Potentially yes, if the patient also has sufficient oral motor function and the other eligibility criteria are met. Patients with enteral feeding for nutritional support are not automatically excluded. However, gastro-oesophageal reflux associated with tube feeding may increase aspiration risk and should be considered in the eligibility evaluation.
